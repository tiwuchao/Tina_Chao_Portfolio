Process

The highway

From wherever a project enters the highway, it drives the same five miles. Each sign is a part of the drive, and each stop gives an example from a case study.

  1. Mile 1Sit with the problemRead, ask, and resist the rush to a solution.

    On the roadAt in2being, the brief said clients didn't value the firm. Sitting with it showed they couldn't see how the work connected.

  2. Mile 2Listen to real peopleResearch from real voices, down to the details people mention in passing.

    On the roadFor LendMate we chose interviews over surveys, because we needed the stories behind the answers.

  3. Mile 3Find the insightThe underlying idea that kept coming back, like a hook—the chorus.

    On the roadMore than ten interviews kept circling the same six kinds of confusion. That became the in2being taxonomy.

  4. Mile 4Admit what I got wrongSay where my first read was off and what changed my mind.

    On the roadRecruiting LendMate alumni through our own networks skewed the sample. Next time I'd recruit wider, earlier.

  5. Mile 5Make it work for themNot what's impressive. Whether it works for the person it was made for.

    On the roadFourth Soil Farm passed on statewide distribution. Growth that didn't fit the founder wasn't a win.

Case study · 2026 in2being

Turning a perception problem into an actionable product strategy

A medical device consultancy whose clients knew only a fraction of what it could do. I turned stakeholder research into a framework the team is now using with clients.

UX research, strategy, interaction design
Timeline

Tools

what I did

Interviewed 10+ stakeholders, built a six-part taxonomy of client confusion, ran a business analysis, and designed two interactive concepts plus a usability plan.

what changed

The founder is using the research to shape client intake, communication and education.

my role

I led the research and strategy work from discovery through recommendation.

Research
Stakeholder interviews, affinity synthesis, perception taxonomy
Strategy

Design
Interactive process tools, value build map
Validation
Usability-testing strategy

“Tina's contributions helped shape an updated marketing approach that we are now implementing.”

Founder, in2being
Mile 1 · The brief

Clients knew a fraction of the firm

in2being is a medical device consultancy that does both product development and regulatory work. It already explained both on its website and in client conversations. Even so, clients didn't consistently understand what the firm could do, and they tended to trust its recommendations only after a different approach had cost them time or money.

So the job wasn't to add more information. It was to make the value that already existed easier to see. To get there, I needed to know:

  • What does the team believe clients misunderstand, and where does it show up?
  • When do clients start to trust in2being's advice, and what gets them there?
  • Is this only a messaging problem, or something in how the business works?
Mile 2 · Listening

Starting inside the firm

Before I could understand clients, I needed to know how the team saw its own work. I ran more than ten stakeholder interviews to map the internal mental model, then grouped the notes in FigJam to find patterns across conversations.

  • Stakeholder interviews10+ conversations across the team
  • Affinity synthesisPatterns across interviews, grouped in FigJam
  • Perception taxonomyThe recurring kinds of client confusion
  • Business analysisBusiness Model Canvas, SWOT, competitor analysis and client segmentation, to test whether this was only a messaging problem

what I heard

  • “They think we only do one of the many things we do — regulatory, or prototype builds, or electronics, or mechanical, or testing.”

  • “They understand a quarter of what we do — Regulatory or Development. They don't understand what that half is needed… if they've had a medical device business [before]. [They] understand half of what your business does, and of that half they understand half of that.”

  • “A lot of our job is in education. Our clients are primarily startups, their budget is very limited… we come from startups so we understand the pressures they're under.”

From stakeholder interviews, as I grouped them in FigJam

Was it only a messaging problem?

To find out, I mapped the business itself: a Business Model Canvas, SWOT, competitive analysis and client segmentation. It wasn't only messaging.

Mile 3 · The insight

Six kinds of confusion, one root cause

The same misunderstandings kept surfacing, interview after interview. I sorted them into six categories the team can use to spot a misaligned client early.

  1. Scope & breadthClients underestimated how much in2being could handle.
  2. Timeline & paceClients didn't understand how the process unfolded.
  3. Process rigorThe depth of the process wasn't obvious.
  4. CommunicationExpertise could read as more technical than approachable.
  5. Budget expectationsProspects formed budgets without seeing the full process.
  6. TerminologyIndustry language added another layer of confusion.

Underneath all six was trust. Clients often didn't recognize or believe in2being's recommendations until another approach had already cost them. That changed the problem statement.

before

Clients don't value the integrated model.

after

Clients may not clearly see how development and regulatory work relate.

Mile 4 · What surprised me

“Clients are confused” wasn't actionable

My first read was that clients were simply confused. But confusion in general doesn't tell anyone what to change.

Naming how they were confused, and when they began to trust in2being, was actionable. The more useful question became what evidence would get clients to that trust sooner.

the traffic drop that wasn't

While reviewing analytics, I found that a dip the team had been reading as lost traffic was really a spike of bot and referral spam in GA4. I flagged it as a data-quality issue so decisions wouldn't rest on bad numbers.

Mile 5 · The design

Making the integrated model visible

research finding

Clients couldn't see where they were in the development process.

design response

I made the process explorable.

Concept 1Client journey tool

A stage-by-stage walk through a device's path, from conceptual design to market entry. Visitors pick their device class and experience level, then see what in2being does, and what's at stake, at their stage. I built it as a working HTML prototype in in2being's brand colors.

  • Nine stages from Conceptual Design to Market Entry
  • Device-class filter for Class I, II and III
  • Technology Readiness Level toggle
  • Experience toggle that switches technical language to plain English

Concept 2Value build map

A bottom-up view that stacks development and regulatory work stage by stage, from concept and feasibility to submission and launch, so they read as one build instead of two separate tracks.

Concept 3Leading with the integrated model

To put the positioning recommendation into practice where prospects first meet the firm, I used AI to help me turn what I heard in my interviews into a homepage concept, the one at the top of this page. It opens with “Your claims determine your design” and walks through a nine-step process from napkin sketch to market entry.

ValidationA usability plan, ready to run

To check whether these ideas actually help, I wrote a perception study that rides along with website usability sessions. Every session opens with an awareness check, asking what the participant thinks in2being does, and closes with one question tailored to their segment.

What I recommended

what the research makes possible

  • Client intake. Use the taxonomy to spot misaligned expectations early.
  • Positioning & messaging. Lead with the integrated model wherever prospects first meet the firm.
  • Client education. Use the six categories to explain scope, timelines, process and terminology.
  • Make the model visible. Use visual and interactive explanations of how the work connects.

supporting recommendations

  • Trust & validation. Surface track-record case studies earlier, tied to timeline, budget and process-rigor concerns.
  • Cautionary proof points. Pair success stories with what happened when a client didn't follow in2being's guidance. Trust doesn't have to be earned the hard way; it can be borrowed from someone else's.
  • Content distribution. Reach beyond referrals, so prospects build trust before a bad vendor experience.

Where it landed

  • A new lens for client intake and shared language for client education

what I'm not claiming

Nothing has launched or been tested yet, so there's no conversion or usability lift to report. My interviews were with in2being's team, so the six categories are the team's view of its clients, not clients in their own words. The usability plan is how clients get heard directly, and the next step is testing whether the framework leads to better-informed conversations and better-fit prospects.

Still on the road: I've stayed on with in2being, contacting companies in its ecosystem database of about 545 organizations to check their listings and build referral relationships.

Reflection

Good solutions don't start with the interface. They start with understanding what people believe, where that belief comes from, and what evidence would be strong enough to change it.

Class project · 2026 Songbird Cafe

Ordering ahead without the guesswork

A responsive redesign of a neighborhood cafe's website, so people can check hours, find dietary options, and order ahead for pickup.

Role
Solo: field research, UX/UI design, prototyping, usability testing
Timeline
September to October 2026 · SI 407 Advanced Design, individual project
Platforms
Responsive website, desktop and mobile
Tools
Figma, Dev Mode, FigJam, paper sketches

Clickable prototype ↗ Figma file ↗ Dev Mode handoff ↗

the problem

Visitors come to the site for one quick answer: hours, dietary options, or what kind of place it is. Those answers are hard to find, and the Toast ordering page lists items under different names than the cafe's menu.

what I did

An on-site visit with 6 intercept interviews (11 adults), a conversation with on-duty staff, and a review analysis. Then 8 homepage sketches, a desktop prototype, 6 mobile wireframes, and a usability test against the live site.

the result

On the live site, 2 of 3 participants couldn't find the sandwich they were asked to order. On my prototype, all 3 went straight to Order Online, and 2 of 3 found the item and its add button right away.

overview

Most people walk in without checking the website. I redesigned it for the people who do.

Mile 1 · The brief

The website doesn't answer the questions people bring to it

Songbird's in-person experience builds loyal regulars, but the site doesn't show the current menu, dietary options, hours, or what kind of place it is. First-timers arrive uncertain, and staff absorb questions the site could answer.

user goals

  • Know whether it's open and how to get there (hours changed this year; parking matters)
  • Find food that fits a dietary need: gluten-free, dairy-free, sugar-free
  • Skip the wait at peak times (staff named an 11:30–12:40 rush)
  • Understand what kind of place it is before visiting

business goals

  • A community hub with loyal, repeat customers
  • Less work for staff
  • Grow existing revenue: online orders through Toast, e-gift cards, wholesale bakery

scope, and how it started

Scope
The homepage and the online ordering flow (menu, item details, cart, checkout, confirmation) on desktop and mobile.
How it started
Songbird was on a class list of local businesses whose websites had room to improve. Songbird didn't ask for a redesign; I chose it because I like going there.
Why ordering
Songbird takes orders through Toast, a third-party platform it can't redesign. My flow is a concept for ordering inside Songbird's own site, built around the lunch rush staff described.
Mile 2 · Listening

Three sources pointed to the same problems

I combined observation, interviews, and online reviews so no single source decided the direction. The reviews confirmed what I heard in person and contradicted none of it.

  • Observation3 timed snapshots during a 2-hour visit. Noise went 5/10 → 2/10 → 7/10, with 6 people in line as lunch began.
  • Intercept interviews6 groups, 11 adults: families, remote workers, first-timers, regulars. Goals, ordering friction, and how people use the website.
  • Conversation with on-duty staff15–20 minutes. The cafe's goals and what staff wish customers knew.
  • Review analysisYelp, Tripadvisor, Facebook, Foursquare, and the current site, to see whether interview findings held up at scale.

Heard in person, echoed in reviews

Mile 3 · The insight

Ordering is easy in person, but the website leaves people guessing

  1. The website is a pre-visit tool, not a habit5 of 6 groups walked in without checking it. People who went online came for one answer: hours, parking, or dietary options.
  2. First-timers are the ones who look aheadThe only group that checked the site was visiting for the first time, and they couldn't find what they wanted.
  3. Dietary options build loyaltyGluten-free and dairy-free regulars named those options as a reason they return.
  4. Small in-person snags add upSilverware you have to ask for, a menu far from the kiosk, drink sizes in ounces.
  5. Unwritten house rules cost staff timeStaff want customers to bus dishes and ask for silverware, but nothing tells them before they arrive.

what I heard

  • “The only times I check the website is to see the hours and whether or not it's open.”

  • “I checked the menu to see if there were sugar-free options. I didn't find it…”

Intercept interviews, Sept 2026

Fix what happens before and during ordering

  • Journey map7 stages from Discovery to Departure for a composite first-timer. Friction clustered at Pre-visit, Ordering, Waiting, and Departure.
  • Product vision boardTarget groups (remote workers, students, families, friends), top needs (a space to work, a social space, gluten-free and dairy-free options), and business goals.
  • BuiltState the concept; keep the menu currentHero names breakfast, lunch, coffee; one menu everywhere

  • BuiltPost a wait estimatePickup estimate on the menu; progress tracker on confirmation
  • BuiltRead the order back before payingItem changes shown in cart and checkout
  • Not builtLive seating or wait statusNeeds live data from the cafe
  • Out of scopeDrink temperature and egg prepKitchen operations, not a website problem
Mile 4 · The design

Every key decision traces back to research

IdeationEight homepage layouts, narrowed by peer critique

I sketched 8 desktop homepage layouts on paper: hero-and-cards, a split image-and-text layout, a logo-centered page with four calls to action, and two experiments built on curved shapes and a timeline.

what 2 peers said

  • A clear hero with cards underneath was easiest to follow
  • The main call to action needed to be more obvious
  • Image-led sections communicated faster than text-heavy ones

what I carried forward

  • Hero followed by card sections (the middle-left sketch)
  • A filled “Order online” primary button, outlined “View menu” secondary
  • Photo-led favorites

Wireframes to high fidelityFrom paper to a small design system

  • Lo-fiPaper sketches of the desktop homepage.
  • Desktop v1 · Oct 13High-fidelity screens for usability testing, built on Songbird's existing brand.
  • Desktop v2 · Oct 20Interactions wired through checkout, alt text, tooltips, accessibility fixes.
  • Mobile mid-fi · Oct 26–27Grayscale frames for the homepage, menu, item sheet, cart, checkout, and confirmation.
  • Mobile hi-fi · Oct 27Color, type, and components applied from the design system.
Type
IBM Plex Serif headings for warmth, Inter for body text and buttons. Separate desktop and mobile scales.
Color
Espresso brown, sage, and neutral ramps that echo the cafe's wood and plants. All text pairs pass WCAG AA (4.5:1).
Layout
Desktop: 1280 px, 12 columns, 24 px gutters, 80 px margins. Mobile: 499 px, 4 columns, 16 px gutters and margins.
Components
Buttons (Type × State × Size), input fields, nav bars, footers, product cards, and an order summary card.

Case study · 2025–26 Argus Farm Stop

Making a farm stop feel easier to choose

Students cared about local food, but convenience, not price, quality, or even sustainability, decided where they shopped. I followed that finding from interviews and usability tests to a journey map, a landing-page redesign and recommendations for Argus, and now a redesign of the whole Argus website.

Tools

Argus had a strong local-food mission and 200+ Michigan farms and food producers on its shelves. But University of Michigan students weren't shopping there.

what I did

I worked with two teammates. We split the interview load, conducted the competitive analysis together, and I independently led the website usability audit and wireframe redesign. In fall 2026 I picked the project back up to redesign the rest of the site.

Mile 1 · The brief

Students weren't shopping there

Argus Farm Stop is a year-round farmers market and café that's been serving Ann Arbor since 2014. Farmers drop off their food, set their own prices and keep 70% of every sale, so Argus can sell local food every day while the farmers stay on the farm. But despite its strong mission, University of Michigan students largely weren't shopping there.

This project started as a class assignment, but I wanted it to mean something beyond the grade. Before we began, I reached out to Argus Farm Stop directly to ask whether this was a problem they were actually facing and whether research like ours could be genuinely useful to them. That conversation shaped everything. It kept our work grounded in real organizational needs rather than hypothetical ones, and it gave me a stakeholder relationship to stay accountable to throughout the process.

Were students avoiding Argus because of affordability, convenience, visibility, or something else? We set out to answer:

  • How do perceptions of affordability, convenience, and visibility influence students' decisions to visit and make purchases at Argus Farm Stop?
  • How might we improve the digital experience for Argus Farm Stop customers so that they can more easily discover products, understand the Farm Stop model, and convert to loyal shoppers?

the constraint

Argus takes orders on separate stores: the Argus Online Market on Localine, Argus Shop, and Square for gift cards. Only argusfarmstop.com was ours to change, so anything that happens inside those stores became a recommendation instead of a design.

Mile 2 · Listening

What we heard from students

We interviewed students, watched others use the website, and compared Argus with five competitors.

  • Usability tests6 students over Zoom, thinking aloud through real tasks on Argus's site
  • Website usability auditA WAVE accessibility check and heuristic review I led independently, benchmarked against Agricole Farm Stop in Chelsea, MI
  • Competitive analysisFive competitors compared on branding, e-commerce features and storytelling: Fresh Thyme, People's Food Co-op and Centre Markets (direct), Instacart (indirect) and Etsy (analogous)
  • Affinity synthesisInterview and session notes grouped into themes in Miro

Patterns that kept appearing

Mile 3 · Watching

Students wanted to support Argus. The website got in the way.

To see whether the website itself was part of the barrier, we ran moderated usability tests over Zoom with six University of Michigan students who grocery shop and had ordered groceries online before. Most were first-time visitors to the Argus site. They thought aloud while completing goal-based tasks, and we logged task success, time, errors, hesitations, and comments for each session. Argus provided $10 gift cards as participant incentives.

three issues came up again and again

  1. Text that looked like links but wasn'tFarm names on product pages were red or bold, so participants tried to click them to find the vendor. One said, “I'm looking for a place to click.”
  2. Getting lost between two sitesShopping happens on Localine, a separate platform with a different look. Participants weren't sure which site they were on or how to get back to the Argus homepage.
  3. Subscriptions hidden from the shopThe produce box and meat subscription weren't in the main catalog. They lived on separate pages with different layouts, so most participants couldn't find them while browsing.

Our clearest takeaway: even students who wanted to support Argus said the experience felt fragmented. One said they'd rather just shop in person.

The audit I led

I checked the existing site with the WAVE accessibility tool and a heuristic review, then benchmarked it against Agricole Farm Stop in Chelsea, MI. Agricole explains what it is in one sentence above the fold, with a five-item menu. Argus took scrolling to explain itself, behind six menu items with dropdowns, and its three main options were illustrated cards that didn't look clickable.

what competitors did better

Say what you are, up front

The strongest sites described the business in the hero and gave a clear, immediate call to action.

Make reordering easy

Buy It Again, Favorites and Save for Later were standard elsewhere and missing from Argus.

Promote what's in season

Banners, deals and seasonal promotions gave shoppers a reason to look again. Argus had little of this.

Put people in the pictures

Grids of farmers, real photos and short stories humanized the people behind the food.

Mile 4 · The insight

Convenience outweighed everything else

Convenience emerged as the single most important factor in students' grocery decisions, outweighing both price and quality. Students shop where they can get in and out quickly, on foot or on their way to class. Even when they were sympathetic to shopping local, most assumed Argus would be more expensive than chain stores, a perception that often wasn't tested. And while students cared about sustainability and community, those values didn't reliably translate into behavior once time, money, and access got in the way.

  1. ConvenienceStudents prioritized getting groceries quickly and easily.
  2. Perceived costMany assumed Argus would be more expensive without actually checking.
  3. Values ≠ behaviorStudents cared about sustainability and community, but those values didn't overcome time and access barriers.
  4. Lack of familiarityThe Farm Stop model itself required explanation.

Meet the person behind the pattern

Sophie doesn't need to be convinced that local food matters. If shopping local requires extra planning, she won't do it consistently. Every participant was a student, so I kept to one persona rather than inventing others the research couldn't support.

Following Sophie through a week

I mapped Sophie's journey from her usual grocery run to whether she comes back the next week, and tagged every pain point with the finding behind it. Her two lowest points both happen on the website: checking whether Argus is worth the trip, and picking how to shop. That's where design could help her most.

Mile 5 · The first design

Making the case before the trip

If convenience was the strongest driver, the website couldn't make students work to understand why Argus was worth the trip.

For the class project, I redesigned the landing-page experience around three things students needed before deciding to visit: what Argus is, why it is worth choosing, and what they can expect when they get there. The redesign is at the top of this page.

  2. Why it's worth choosingFarm stories, sourcing and community impact
  3. What to expectHours, locations, pickup and delivery

Design responseA landing page built on what we heard

Informed directly by our research insights, I designed a conversion-focused landing page applying CRO (Conversion Rate Optimization) principles. Key design decisions included:

  • A clear value proposition above the fold that explained the Farm Stop model in plain language
  • Trust signals (farm stories, sourcing transparency, community impact) to build credibility with first-time visitors
  • Streamlined navigation reducing cognitive load for mobile users
  • Strong calls to action tied to identified customer motivations

  2. Brand positioningLead with what makes Argus different: a market and café where you can meet the farmers who grow your food, with a clear tagline, photos of real producers and customers, and a simple explanation of the Farm Stop model.
  3. Customer acquisition strategyAwareness spreads mostly through word of mouth. Reach students with visible promotions and seasonal items, the café and ready-to-eat food they already look for, and proof that pickup and SNAP make Argus more accessible than they assume.

Case study · 2024 LendMate Alum

Finding out who would actually lend

Before a lending platform got built, our research gave its founders their first evidence-based view of the alumni who would fund it.

Client
LendMate, an early-stage startup
Role
UX researcher
Timeline
February to April 2024 · class project
Team
Five student researchers · 30 alumni interviews, 7+ run by me
Tools
Miro, Canva

the problem

LendMate had a compelling pitch for students and no validated picture of the alumni who would fund the loans.

what I did

Ran 7+ of our team's 30 alumni interviews, synthesized the notes in affinity diagrams, and built two lender personas and journey maps.

what changed

The founders got two distinct kinds of lender to design for, backed by evidence instead of a hunch.

“We couldn't assume. We had to ask.”

How our team started
Mile 1 · The context

An unproven lender side

LendMate is building peer-to-peer lending between a university's alumni and its current students. Alumni lend directly, earn a return and support their school: personal, flexible and good for both sides, in the way Airbnb is.

Our class worked as LendMate's research team. Alumni would be putting real money into a personal loan for a stranger, so we needed to know:

  • Who would actually lend?
  • What would motivate them, and what would make them hesitate?
  • How would their relationship with their university shape the decision?

what I owned

  • Alumni interviews
  • Interview guide
  • Synthesis
  • Affinity diagrams
  • Lender personas
  • Journey maps
  • Founder presentation
Mile 2 · Listening

Interviews, not surveys

We were trying to discover hypotheses, not confirm them. A survey can't tell you why someone trusts or distrusts a financial platform, so we chose in-depth interviews. Our team of five interviewed 30 alumni, and I ran at least seven of those interviews myself.

  • One interviewer, one note-takerSo the person asking could stay fully present in the conversation.
  • Open, non-leading questionsHow alumni feel about their university as adults, how they think about money they've already earned, and what giving back means to them.
  • Recruiting the hard wayUniversity email lists and alumni groups went mostly unanswered, so we turned to personal networks. That kept the project moving, and it introduced sampling bias.
  • Affinity diagrams in MiroWe coded the notes and clustered them until the patterns across participants were clear.
Mile 3 · The insight

Two reasons to lend

One onboarding flow would not serve both kinds of lender.

The two archetypes weren't just age groups. They differed in motivation, risk tolerance and what they expected LendMate to be.

the middle-aged investor

Financially established and engaged with the university. Motivated by return, credibility and building a legacy. Thinks in portfolios, so LendMate has to feel structured and trustworthy.

the retired philanthropist

Less focused on returns, more on community impact and helping the next generation. The human story matters as much as the financial terms.

Mile 4 · What it meant

What it meant for LendMate

  • Trust has to be built two waysInvestors need confidence in the financial structure and the returns.
  • The value can't only be financialPhilanthropic lenders need to see the human and community impact.
  • There is no single lender mindsetThe product experience should reflect both motivations.

We mapped each lender's journey through awareness and consideration, marking where their expectations and worries split apart and where LendMate could improve.

Mile 5 · Where it landed

Where it landed

At the end of the semester we presented our findings and journey maps to LendMate's founders. They were satisfied, and the research can inform a real product build.

We also delivered a mock investor pitch deck that turned the research into a business story, showing that UX insight can speak beyond the design team.

what I'd do differently

Recruiting through our own networks gave us people who already trusted us, which likely tilted answers toward optimism. Next time I would recruit a more varied group earlier, build in incentives from the start, and give synthesis more time. The notes held more than we had time to surface.

Thank you to Professor Natalie Fang, the LendMate founders and my team.

Reflection

The best moments came from restraint: not leading, not filling the silence, and letting people keep talking.
